      Argonaut Web — user guide

      The war at a glance

      Argonaut Web is a turn-based fleet battle in deep space. You command the Federation flagship Argo — and through it the whole Federation — against three enemy alliances. Every stardate you issue one command; then every other captain in the war zone acts at once. The war ends when one side is annihilated, when every survivor is stranded beyond reach of anything, or when you resign and let the autopilots finish what you started.

      • Four alliances field five hulls each — a battle cruiser, three cruisers, and a scout — plus the Federation starbase Xanadu, which fights at your side.
      • One command spends one stardate. Information commands, fleet orders, and refits are free.
      • The war autosaves after every action; reload the page and resume where you left off.
      • The seed shown in the top bar fixes the opening disposition. Same seed, same war.

      Starting a war

      New game in the top bar opens the war panel. Name a seed or take the one offered; tick the options you want. A classic war with every box unticked plays exactly by the original 1992 rules — the flags only add.

      • Regional enemy formations starts the enemy fleets clustered by alliance instead of scattered.
      • Tactical sound adds phaser, photon, explosion, and miss effects, plus a klaxon on the way into RED alert.
      • Precision fire gives your phaser volleys a power dial and called subsystem shots.
      • Extended war adds standing orders for your fleet, alliance doctrines, the Xanadu dockyard, and three scenarios.
      • Argonaut Reimagined is an expansion mode under active development — it carries the extended war and adds a wider 320-unit battlefield with a pan/zoom camera, reactor power management, the directed tractor beam, and a living battlefield of nebulae, asteroid fields, and ion storms. See Argonaut Reimagined below. A classic or extended war is untouched.

      Reading the tactical map

      • Every hull is a circle bearing its initial, coloured by alliance; a wreck is a +. A dashed border means vacant or surrendered.
      • Dashed rings around your ship mark phaser (red), photon (yellow), and engine (cyan) reach. In an extended war the green ring around Xanadu is the dockyard.
      • A red outline marks an enemy that can reach you this stardate.
      • The legend under the map is the color key for everything drawn on it — alliances, your range rings, threats, wrecks — and it grows with the war mode: the orders pip, ace star, and dockyard ring in an extended war; the terrain hues (nebula, asteroids, ion storm, relay nodes) and the prize pip in a Reimagined one.
      • The map is limited by your mapper — fog of war. Backspace shows the whole war zone; 7 lists exact local positions.
      • Click empty space to steer your ship toward that point, clamped to your engine ring. Click a hull for its context menu: every command your ship can actually perform on it, and nothing else. Escape puts the menu away.

      Commands and keys

      Every command has a console button and a key. The numbered commands spend your stardate; the information commands at the bottom are free and print into the mission panel.

      3 PhasersEnergy volley at a hull in range; scales with live phaser units.
      4 PhotonsTorpedo at close range; heavier damage, smaller reach.
      5 TractorLock a beam and drag the target toward you.
      6 ScannerScan a hull. In an extended war, reveals who captains it.

      ` AutopilotYour ship fights one turn like an enemy captain — ruthless, but clumsy.
      Esc ResignHand the whole Federation to the autopilot and watch the war play out. Final.

      Hyperspace, self-destruct, and resign ask for confirmation first — none of them can be undone. While any dialog is open it owns the keyboard, so game keys never fire behind a prompt.

      Combat and survival

      • Battles are attritional. Hulls carry double the shields and crew the gunnery table was tuned against: a kill takes some fourteen volleys, a war about twenty-five stardates. The guns themselves are unchanged from the original.
      • Shots miss — yours and the enemy's alike — and every volley rolls its own damage band.
      • Damage past your shields knocks out subsystems, shortening scanner, mapper, transporter, and radio reach. Lose all crew and the hull sits vacant, boardable by your transporter — command included.
      • Tractor locks pull both ways and end only when the holding ship dies; hyperspace also shakes one off. A held hull wears a cyan pip and the console names the holder — or says an unseen hull when a nebula hides it, since the beam is felt even when the ship casting it cannot be seen.
      • Two hulls on the same point collide: one is destroyed, the other crippled. This applies to your moves, the autopilots', tractor tows, and hyperspace arrivals.
      • Lose your command ship or resign and command shifts to another Federation hull; the war goes on without you.
      • Enemy fleets concentrate fire on a shared target in a classic war; the vendetta ship breaks formation to hunt you personally.

      The extended war

      An extended war keeps every original rule and adds an admiral's layer: standing orders for your fleet, a dockyard at Xanadu, alliance doctrines, named captains, and three scenarios. Orders are free and persist until changed; they travel by radio, so a hull out of contact acknowledges one stardate late.

      • Focus with fleet (original behavior), Hold, Withdraw toward Xanadu, Escort a friendly hull, Screen a ward against the nearest threat, Intercept one named enemy.
      • Xanadu is a dockyard. A hull ending the stardate inside the green ring recovers shields, crew, and one unit of its worst-damaged subsystem — and may take one refit per war: an extra photon bay, overcharged phasers, a tuned drive, or deeper sensors.
      • Each alliance fights its own way — Axis swarms and detonates rather than die, Bloc works the phaser edge and executes the weakest hull it can hit, Cabal tractors you into collisions, Federation concentrates fire with damage discipline.
      • Earn the captains' names. A named captain has sworn to hunt you; scanning hulls reveals who commands what. Two kills make a captain an ace (★ on the map), and every third kill your hunter scores makes its volleys against you bite harder.
      • Replay the round from the battle narrative header to watch the whole computer phase play back on the map.
      • Three scenarios: cease hostilities (the original), hold Xanadu to stardate 30, or hunt the hunter — identify your vendetta captain by scanning before the war kills them first.

      Precision fire

      With the flag on, every phaser prompt carries two dials. A called system spends 40% of the rolled volley on one subsystem with no crew casualties — strip shields with standard fire first, then burn out engines or guns and leave the hull intact. The power dial throttles the volley: a measured finish never carries the overkill that shatters a hull, so it leaves a boardable prize instead of wreckage.

      A hull with crew left but no engines, phasers, or photons strikes its colors at stardate end — crew away in escape pods, hull adrift for your transporter. The enemy still fires standard volleys either way.

      Argonaut Reimagined

      Argonaut Reimagined is an opt-in expansion mode, chosen in the New game panel and still under active development. It carries the whole extended war — fleet orders, alliance doctrines, the Xanadu dockyard, named captains, and the scenarios — and layers original systems on top. A classic or extended war is untouched: every Reimagined addition is read only when the flag is on, so the calibrated 1992 game is preserved exactly. More systems land over time.

      A wider battlefield. A Reimagined war opens on a 320-unit field instead of 100, so there is room to screen, flank, and disengage. Weapon ranges stay at their classic units while engine reach scales with the field, so a hull crosses the wider map in about the same number of stardates but its guns cover a smaller fraction of it. Hulls that would end a move stacked on the same point as another hull — drone wings above all — fan out to the nearest free point, while tractor slams, hyperspace landings, and rams you order yourself still collide; and hulls sharing a point draw fanned apart so every glyph stays seeable and clickable.

      • The map is a viewport into that field: wheel zooms toward the cursor, arrow keys pan, and the minimap (bottom-right) shows the whole war zone with your viewport rectangle — drag it to re-center.
      • The camera follows your flagship until you pan or zoom; the ⌖ button snaps back onto it, and + / − zoom in and out.

      Reactor power. Every Reimagined hull runs a damageable reactor whose live units set a power budget you distribute across five sinks — shields, weapons, engines, sensors, and tractor — from the power bar in the command console. Each row shows its allocation, a live effectiveness multiplier, and −/+ pips; the header reads spent / budget.

      • Setting power is free (it costs no stardate) and persists across the autosave. Each sink runs at its calibrated level (1.00×) by default.
      • Overcharge one sink and you must starve another — the budget is fixed and a sink saturates at 1.5×, so it is a real trade, not a free boost. Weapons scale volley damage, engines scale movement reach, sensors scale scanner/mapper/radio/transporter range, tractor scales beam pull, and surplus shield power regenerates shields each stardate.
      • The reactor is damageable: a precision called shot can knock it out, shrinking that hull's budget and everything it powers. The dockyard repairs reactor damage, and a one-time Reactor upgrade refit adds a unit, raising the budget.
      • Enemy captains run doctrine power profiles — Axis biases guns and speed, Bloc guns and sensors (and never tractors), Cabal speed and tractor, Federation a balanced shields lean. Your own command ship stays neutral until you move the bar.

      Directed tractor beam. Open an enemy inside tractor range and choose Direct tow… to aim the beam at a coordinate or another hull instead of reeling the target straight toward you. The victim is hauled along that vector for the same pull budget — you choose the direction, not the distance — so towing an enemy into another hull is a deliberate tractor-ram.

      A living battlefield. Each war seeds terrain across the wide field — violet nebulae, slate asteroid fields, and amber ion storms — drawn as translucent overlays on the tactical map and the minimap. Terrain is known geography: it renders crisp while your mapper can reach it and faint beyond, so you can always route by the field's shape.

      • A hull lurking inside a nebula is hidden from an outside mapper, scanner, and radio — however far your sensors reach — beyond a short reveal range at the nebula's edge. Sharing the nebula sees it plainly.
      • The reveal range scales with your sensors effectiveness: overcharging the sensors sink pierces deeper into a nebula, at the cost of whatever you starve to do it — the counter to nebula camping.
      • Radio into a nebula degrades the same way, so a hull camped inside one also misses orders until they travel late.
      • Asteroid fields give cover: any volley — yours or the enemy's — whose shot line crosses a field can splash on a rock instead of hitting. Firing from inside the field is no safer than firing across it.
      • A hull that ends a move or a tractor tow inside an asteroid field risks a rock strike that rakes its shields; passing straight through at speed is safe. That makes a directed tow into the rocks a deliberate weapon — and gives the engines sink a defensive use, since outrunning the field means never parking inside it.
      • Ion storms jam constantly, in two zones. In the core a hull's weapons and radio are offline for the stardate: it cannot fire — the command simply refuses — and it neither sends nor hears radio, so orders to it arrive late. Engines, sensors, and tractor work throughout, so a caught hull can flee or be towed out.
      • In a storm's outer ring the jam is only partial: volleys fired from inside miss more often, and radio reach halves. Predictable to plan around — you can fight at a penalty, or skim a message through.
      • Two relay nodes sit mirrored off Xanadu as capturable objectives, drawn as small hard-ringed markers. A hull that ends a stardate inside one — and is not tractor-held — holds it for its alliance: every hull of that alliance gains +5 reactor budget while the node is held, enough to overcharge a sink without starving another.
      • Contesting a node (two alliances inside at stardate end) darkens it, and driving the holder off frees it. A held node wears its alliance's colored ring on the map and its colors on the minimap — ownership is public knowledge, so the nodes are where the war gets decided.

      A prize fleet. A hull whose crew dies but whose frame survives is a prize waiting to be taken, and in a Reimagined war every capture is recorded: the Fleet orders report and the ship's menu tell you where the hull came from, when it was taken, and how many hands are aboard, and a prize on the map wears a gold pip.

      • Any Federation hull can be ordered to Board… a derelict: it sails to within transporter range and beams a prize crew over on its own. Your command ship's transporter (8) still boards directly.
      • A freshly taken prize is under-manned — below a quarter of its complement its engines and guns run at half effectiveness — and it automatically withdraws toward Xanadu. Dock it, or beam crew across, to bring it up to strength before ordering it into the line.
      • Enemy captains take prizes too: with no shot to fire, any captain near a derelict puts a prize crew aboard. Knocking a hull out precisely is now a race — disable it, or someone else will.
      • A prize whose own crew dies goes dark again and can be retaken by anyone, including its original alliance. Boarding an alliance's last hull ends that alliance's war.

      New hull classes. A Reimagined war fields a bigger navy than the canonical 21: fleets can include three classes beyond the classic five. The interceptor is the fastest hull on the field (seven engine units), with light guns, thin shields, and a skeleton complement; a glass raider that wins by speed, screening and running down derelicts and being somewhere else when the volleys land. The artillery ship is its opposite: two engine units that hold the phaser edge rather than chase, and six phaser banks throwing the hardest volley of any warship — sturdy but unquick, a sitting duck for anything faster that gets inside its guns. And the carrier is a slow, sturdy tender built for the prize fleet: the longest boarding arm of any hull that moves (forty units), a starbase's tow for hauling prizes and wreck-rams, modest guns of its own, and the biggest crew pool afloat after Xanadu to man the prizes it takes — plus the drone bay below.

      Fighter drones. A carrier carries one complement of three fighter drones for the whole war. Flying the carrier, Launch drones (D) spends the stardate to put the wing on the field; any carrier can instead take the Launch drones standing order, which looses the bay automatically when an enemy closes — and enemy carriers launch on the same trigger. The wing is drawn small on the map, always glyphed D, and named after its carrier (Lexington D1, D2, D3).

      • Drones are fast, light gunboats with nobody aboard: an interceptor's near-speed, a phaser pair, paper shields. They escort their carrier, intercepting anything that closes on it, and can be given standing orders of their own like any Federation hull.
      • A drone is never a prize — there is no crew to kill and no hull to board — and no crew can be beamed onto one. When its carrier is destroyed the wing fights on alone, hunting the nearest enemy until shot down.
      • Drones count for nothing in the endgame: an alliance down to drones is out of the war, and its wing goes dark with its last crewed hull. The bay is never rebuilt — not even at the Xanadu dockyard, which drones never dock at. A captured carrier keeps its complement, and the drones fly for whoever flies her.

      Combat stances. Every Reimagined hull holds one of three stances — a free, persistent choice like reactor power. Set your command ship's from the console Combat stance control, or any Federation hull's from its menu. Standard is neutral. Firing steadies the guns: your volleys are more accurate, but you are not maneuvering, so incoming fire finds you more easily. Evasive weaves: incoming fire misses more often, but your own shots are thrown off. The stance shows as a colored halo on the map (warm for firing, cold for evasive), in the fleet report, and on an enemy's menu, and it stacks with asteroid cover and ion-storm static on the one shared accuracy roll. Enemy captains hold a stance by doctrine — the Axis swarm and the Bloc artillery line fire, Cabal tricksters evade — and any hull beaten low weaves as it breaks off.

      Disengage. The Disengage command (X) is the pilot's escape lever: a full engine burn straight away from the nearest threat, computed for you rather than clicked. It is your maneuver for the stardate — it spends the turn like any move and can still collide or take a rock strike on arrival — so pair it with the evasive stance (set evasive for free, then disengage) to break off under fire.

      Ion / EMP. Some hulls — artillery and interceptors — carry an ion emitter (I, or Fire ion from an enemy's menu), a long-range suppression beam that outranges the phasers. It disables rather than destroys: the target's shields absorb the charge first, and whatever punches through burns out subsystem units without killing a single crewman. Gut a hull's engines and guns and it strikes its colors at the stardate's end, left a vacant derelict for anyone to board — so ion is a hunter's tool that feeds the prize race instead of sending hulls to wreckage. It rides the same accuracy roll as the guns (stances and terrain apply), scales with the weapons power sink, and the dockyard rebuilds what it burns out.

      Spread torpedoes. The heaviest hulls — battle cruisers and carriers — carry spread torpedo tubes (T, or Fire spread from an enemy's menu), a short-range area salvo. The target you name takes the full blast, and every hull near the impact takes a share that falls off with distance. The splash is indiscriminate — your own wingmen can be caught if they are near the impact (the shooter spares only itself) — so it is a deliberate counter to tight formations and a risk in a melee, exactly the clustering a last-stand blast punishes. A salvo that misses splashes nothing. It rides the same accuracy roll as the guns, and enemy captains loose it only into a clean, clustered splash. The impact draws a ring the true size of the splash.

      Directional shields. A Reimagined ship of the line carries its shields across four weighted arcs — fore, starboard, aft, port; the bow is the thickest and the stern the thinnest — and a heading, drawn as a needle on the map. An aimed volley (phasers, photons, a spread salvo's primary hit) burns the arc it strikes first; part of the overflow bleeds into the other arcs, and the rest reaches crew and systems at once — so a gutted flank lets the enemy through early, fighting bow-on matters, disengaging exposes your weak aft to pursuers, and crossing behind a runner is a killing angle. Any engine burn sets the heading; the Helm control (console, or any Federation hull's menu) turns 45° either way for free, without spending the stardate, and enemy captains keep their bows on you by doctrine. Blasts, collisions, rock strikes, ion bursts, and spread splash on secondary hulls are positional — they burn every arc in proportion. Shield focus (also free) routes recovery — reactor regen, the dockyard, an engine flush — into one arc first; left on auto, the weakest arc refills first. The arc breakdown reads on the console, in every hull's menu, in the scan report, and in the fleet report. Drones are too small for arcs, and weapons stay all-directional.

      Fleet loadout. A Reimagined war's fleets are composed, not fixed. Tick Reimagined in the New game panel and the fleet loadout appears: spend your points budget (24 by default) on ships of the line — the flagship battle cruiser is required, and carriers, artillery, cruisers, interceptors, and scouts cost 4 / 3 / 2 / 2 / 1, up to eight hulls. You also set every alliance's budget: enemy captains draw seeded fleets within theirs, leaning to their doctrines — Axis gunboat swarms, Bloc artillery lines, Cabal carriers — so the same seed replays the same war, and a new seed fields new navies. Prizes taken in the war are won, not budgeted.

      Force customization. The same panel chooses who fights — any two to four alliances, though the Federation always flies and at least one enemy must — and whether Xanadu spawns. Without the starbase there is no dockyard, no radio relay, and withdraw runs to the fleet centroid; the computer report simply says the distance to Xanadu is unknown, and the relay nodes still sit mirrored around where the base would have stood. Hold Xanadu needs its base, so that scenario is unavailable with the starbase off — and the rules force it back on if the war somehow starts that way.

      Random encounters. The war's back half is not an empty field. Most stardates are quiet, but now and then something arrives from outside the battlefield, at least 40 units from any hull: a derelict — a ghost ship of some alliance, dark, degraded, and vacant, which anyone can board for a prize (enemy captains race you to them); a distress call — a stranded Federation hull with its engines burnt out, marked by a slow amber blinker, which you can tow home to the dockyard (or transfer crew to) to return it to the fight; or a neutral merchant — an unarmed civilian hull in gray that runs from any warship that closes and jumps out after a bounded visit. A merchant is no one's enemy: captains ignore them, they never count toward victory or surrender, and a relay node cannot be held by one. But a transporter party across seizes it whole — hull, cargo, and crew, as a prize of war. You can also simply shoot one down; nothing prices that yet.

      The sector campaign. Tick Sector campaign in the New game panel (it carries Reimagined) and the war becomes a campaign across a star map: a seeded corridor of systems from Xanadu to the enemy home, five columns deep, with two or three routes per hop. On the sector screen you click a system to read it and travel along the bright routes; at an enemy system you choose Engage to fight the battle yourself or Auto-resolve to let your captains fight it headless. Every node battle is a full Reimagined war — the garrison is drawn within the system's budget, deeper columns fight harder — and what survives carries out: wounds, subsystem burns, prizes, captains, and aces all persist, while orders, power, and stances reset for the next engagement. Winning takes the system and pays credits; a captured hull pays a one-time class bounty when it is carried out; and between battles, at Xanadu or any captured system, the dockyard spends credits on shield and crew repairs, system overhauls, re-purchasable refits, drone-bay rebuilds, and new commissions against the loadout's point budget — carried prizes never count against it. A hopeless draw or an Abandon engagement cedes a system and lets you move on; losing every hull ends the campaign, and taking the enemy home system wins it. The enemy alliances make their own moves as the campaign turns: they contest each other's systems, seize empty ones, and raid yours — a raid that reaches the system your fleet holds (or Xanadu itself, which recalls the fleet) becomes a defensive battle you play or auto-resolve, and losing Xanadu ends the campaign, while raids elsewhere are beaten off (or not) by the garrison headless. The side panel's campaign report grades the run as it goes — battles by outcome, systems held, the credit ledger, prizes, bounties, and aces. The campaign autosaves under its own slot, and the same seed replays the same sector, the same battles, and the same outcome.

      Reimagined is balanced by whole-war simulation as it matures, not calibrated against the 1992 original, so enemy strength and war length can differ from a classic war. The build roadmap lives in the repository under docs/superpowers/specs.

      Comforts and access

      • Classic view in the top bar switches to the black phosphor CRT theme with scanlines; your choice persists between visits.
      • Save / resume — the war lives in your browser's local storage and resumes on reload. New game starts fresh.
      • Motion and impact effects honor prefers-reduced-motion; ships glide between stardates and leave fading trails otherwise.
      • A single concise status line serves screen readers instead of announcing the whole board on every keystroke.

      Provenance, maintenance, and contact

      The original Argonaut is a tactical space-war game for DOS, released in 1992 and originally (c) 1988, 1992 by Jim Chen. This web remake is newly written HTML, CSS, and JavaScript; the supplied DOS manual was used only as a behavioral reference, and the program includes no original .COM, screenshots, or other original assets. Captain names, fleet orders, alliance doctrines, the Xanadu dockyard, the scenarios, and the extended-war narrative are original to this remake.

      The remake is maintained today by MattyGPT as an open-source project. The full source, issue tracker, and release history live in the public repository, and the game you are playing is deployed straight from it:

      • github.com/MattyGPT/Argonaut-web — source, history, and deployment.
      • Open an issue — bug reports, questions, calibration notes against the original, and ideas are all welcome there.
